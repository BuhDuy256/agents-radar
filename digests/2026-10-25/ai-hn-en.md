### Claude proved security properties at the assembly level in 123 open-source libraries
In a collaboration with Theorem, Anthropic says it used Claude to formally verify security properties at the RISC-V assembly level across 123 open-source libraries, proving programs exit normally without memory-safety errors or failed assertions for inputs under 64 MB. The company calls it the first large-scale effort to apply formal verification to production software at the assembly level. Claude also found bugs that had survived years of testing. The proofs assume a single-threaded static binary on a fixed-memory RISC-V CPU, so the result does not cover all possible bugs. A subset of proofs and the RISC-V and OS semantics were published for reuse.

### Paper finds AI-generated text uses harder-to-process language than human writing
A paper by Thomas Hikaru Clark, Matias B. Martin and Kyle Mahowald analyzed psycholinguistic features tied to reading difficulty in a corpus of AI-generated text across domains. Compared with human writing, all nine frontier and near-frontier models studied used more inanimate subjects with animate verbs and more low-frequency n-grams, and most used longer dependencies, stacked nouns and reduced object relatives. The authors propose that human language is optimized for communication while AI text is optimized for goals like math, code and reasoning, where information density matters more. They show a fine-tuned model under a length penalty diverges from human-like language. The finding is corpus-based and does not establish that any one model is better overall.

### EPFL's LogSync chip decodes communication between brain regions at under 200 microwatts
EPFL researchers built LogSync, a 4x2 mm implantable system-on-chip that records brain activity, extracts biomarkers for oscillation strength and synchrony between brain regions, and uses a machine-learning algorithm to classify cognitive and emotional states and trigger stimulation. The work, published in the IEEE Journal of Solid-State Circuits, was funded by a CHF 6.6 million NIMH grant for implantable systems targeting severe psychiatric illness. The chip keeps power under 200 microwatts. It was evaluated on existing human brain recordings and in rat experiments at the University of Minnesota; psychiatric use in humans has not been demonstrated.
